- **Timestamp Converter** turns a Unix timestamp into a date and a date back into a Unix timestamp, both boxes live as you type. Every moment is given in UTC and on your own clock, each with its weekday and how long ago it was, alongside ISO 8601, seconds and milliseconds — all copyable, since the reason for looking one up is that it has to go somewhere else.
- **It will not guess whether your number is seconds or milliseconds.** The same ten digits are a moment in 2026 read as seconds and a moment three weeks into 1970 read as milliseconds, so the unit is a control you set. When the size of the number disagrees with it, the page says what the other unit would read and leaves your setting alone — because a converter that quietly overrules you is one you cannot use to confirm that some other system got the unit wrong.
- **A written date gets the same treatment.** `2026-09-07 14:30` is two different instants in London and Bucharest, so which clock it was read off is a choice too, with your device's offset shown on the control. An offset written into the text — `Z`, `+02:00`, `-0500` — is used instead, and the page names the offset it used.
- **Impossible dates are refused by name.** There is no month 13; February 2026 has 28 days, so there is no 30th; a day runs 00 to 23; and `23:59:60` is a leap second, which is real and which no calendar here can hold. Nothing is parsed by the browser's own date reader, whose idea of a valid date differs between browsers, so the answer does not change with the browser.
- Timestamps before 1970 work in both directions, and the range runs to the year 275760 at either end, which is as far as a date reaches in a browser.
- Your dates are converted on this device, by its own clock. Nothing is sent anywhere and no time server is contacted.
- **The date sentences are now written once.** JWT Decoder and Timestamp Converter both have to render a moment in UTC and say how far away it is, so `UTC` rendering and the `in 3 days` / `2 hours ago` wording moved into one module both read — which is why a token's expiry and a converted timestamp are worded identically rather than nearly so.
